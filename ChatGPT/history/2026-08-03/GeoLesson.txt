New Hampshire lies in northern New England. It borders Canada on the north, Maine and a short stretch of the Atlantic Ocean on the east, Massachusetts on the south, and Vermont on the west. The Connecticut River forms most of the Vermont boundary. The Merrimack River drains much of the center and south, while the Androscoggin and Saco drain parts of the north and east. Glaciers shaped the state's many lakes, including Lake Winnipesaukee, and left thin, stony soils across much of the uplands.

The White Mountains dominate northern New Hampshire. Mount Washington, the highest peak in the northeastern United States, rises there to more than six thousand two hundred feet. The state has warm, humid summers and cold, snowy winters. The Atlantic moderates the small coastal area, while elevation makes the mountains colder and wetter. Rugged northern terrain and extensive forests keep settlement relatively sparse there and support forestry, skiing, hiking, and tourism. Most residents and larger cities, including Manchester and Nashua, are concentrated in the lower, more accessible south near Massachusetts. Concord is the capital.

Today's country is Lithuania.

Lithuania is a Northern European country on the eastern shore of the Baltic Sea. It borders Latvia, Belarus, Poland, and Russia's Kaliningrad region. The land is mostly low and gently rolling because continental ice sheets shaped it, leaving sandy plains, wetlands, forests, and thousands of lakes. The Nemunas is the largest river. It flows west toward the Curonian Lagoon, a shallow body of water separated from the Baltic by the long, dune-covered Curonian Spit.

Lithuania has four distinct seasons and a cool temperate climate. Baltic influence makes the coast milder and windier, while the interior has colder winters and somewhat warmer summers. Fertile lowlands support grain, dairy, and livestock farming, and forests provide timber. Vilnius, the capital and largest city, lies inland in the southeast where the Neris and Vilnia rivers meet. Kaunas grew at the meeting of the Nemunas and Neris, while Klaipėda developed as the country's principal seaport on the narrow passage between the Curonian Lagoon and the Baltic. This short coastline gives Lithuania direct access to maritime trade despite its largely continental landscape.
