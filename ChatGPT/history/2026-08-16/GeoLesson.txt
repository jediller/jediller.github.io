Nebraska.

Nebraska lies in the central Great Plains. South Dakota borders it on the north, Iowa and Missouri on the east, Kansas on the south, Colorado on the southwest, and Wyoming on the west. The Missouri River forms most of the eastern boundary. The Platte River and its tributaries cross the state from west to east through a broad valley that long served as a route for wildlife, Indigenous travel, westward migration, railroads, highways, and cities. Elevation generally rises toward the drier High Plains in the west.

The north-central Sand Hills are one of the world's largest regions of grass-stabilized sand dunes. Their porous soils feed lakes, wetlands, streams, and the Ogallala Aquifer. Nebraska has a continental climate with hot summers, cold winters, strong winds, and frequent drought and severe storms. Rainfall is greater in the east than in the west. Aquifer and river irrigation support corn, soybeans, and cattle feeding, while the Sand Hills favor ranching. Omaha and Lincoln grew in the more densely settled east, where water, rail connections, and productive farmland support trade, food processing, government, and education.

Lesotho.

Lesotho is a small, landlocked country completely surrounded by South Africa. It is the only independent country whose entire territory lies more than one thousand meters above sea level. The western lowlands rise eastward into the rugged Maloti and Drakensberg highlands. Thabana Ntlenyana, about three thousand four hundred eighty-two meters high, is the highest point in southern Africa. Mountain streams feed the Senqu, known downstream as the Orange River, and the Mohokare, also called the Caledon River.

Elevation gives Lesotho cool summers and cold winters, with snow common in the highlands and most rain falling in summer. Steep slopes, thin soils, and heavy grazing make erosion a serious problem. Most people live in the flatter western lowlands near Maseru, where transport and farming are easier. Families grow maize, sorghum, and wheat, while upland herders raise sheep and goats for wool and mohair. The highlands store water that Lesotho transfers to South Africa through dams and tunnels, while also producing hydroelectric power. Mountain terrain makes roads and services costly and leaves many communities relatively isolated.
