Today's state is Louisiana.

Louisiana lies on the Gulf of Mexico. Texas borders it to the west, Arkansas to the north, and Mississippi to the east. Much of the south is a low plain built from sediment carried by the Mississippi River and its distributaries. The river passes Baton Rouge and New Orleans before spreading through a broad delta. The Atchafalaya Basin is the nation's largest river-bottomland hardwood swamp, while bayous, marshes, barrier islands, and coastal bays create a water-rich landscape.

The north has older terraces and rolling uplands. Louisiana's humid subtropical climate brings hot summers, mild winters, heavy rain, and hurricanes. Floods, storm surge, subsidence, erosion, and rising seas continually reshape the coast. Wetlands absorb storm energy and shelter fish and shellfish, but much of the coast is losing land where sediment no longer rebuilds marshes fast enough.

The Mississippi and Gulf made New Orleans and Baton Rouge major port and industrial centers. Energy, refining, shipping, fishing, crawfish farming, rice, sugarcane, forestry, and tourism depend on the state's waters and lowlands. Levees protect communities and navigation but alter the movement of water and sediment. Roads, pipelines, and settlements cross soft, flood-prone ground, making coastal restoration and storm protection central to life in Louisiana.

Today's country is Armenia.

Armenia is a landlocked country in the South Caucasus. Georgia lies to the north, Azerbaijan to the east, Iran to the south, and Turkey to the west. The country is mostly mountainous and averages roughly eighteen hundred meters above sea level. Mount Aragats, an extinct volcanic massif, is the highest point. Volcanic plateaus, steep valleys, and fast rivers leave limited flat land. High-altitude Lake Sevan stores water, supports fishing and recreation, and feeds the Hrazdan River.

The Aras River traces much of Armenia's southern and western frontier and drains fertile valleys where irrigation supports fruit, vegetables, grains, and vineyards. The continental climate brings hot, dry summers in lower valleys and cold, snowy winters at higher elevations. Rainfall varies with elevation and slope. Earthquakes, drought, erosion, and pressure on Lake Sevan are persistent concerns.

Yerevan dominates population and economic life in the Aras basin, while other towns follow valleys that provide rare corridors through the mountains. Copper and gold mining, food and beverage production, technology, manufacturing, agriculture, and tourism are important. Armenia's landlocked position makes road and rail links through neighboring countries essential for trade. Mountain terrain raises transport costs, but the country's highlands, monasteries, lake, and dramatic volcanic scenery also support tourism and a strong regional identity.
