California.

California occupies much of the Pacific coast of the western United States. Oregon is to the north, Nevada and Arizona are to the east, Mexico is to the south, and the Pacific Ocean is to the west. Its land rises from coastal plains and the Coast Ranges to the broad Central Valley and the high Sierra Nevada. Mount Whitney is the highest point in the contiguous United States. Farther south and east lie the Mojave and Colorado deserts, while the volcanic Cascade Range reaches the state's northeast.

The Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers drain the Central Valley and meet in a large delta before reaching San Francisco Bay. Winter rain and Sierra snowpack supply farms and cities through reservoirs and aqueducts, making water storage and allocation central to life in the state. Most coastal areas have dry summers and mild, wet winters; mountains are colder and snowier, and the deserts are extremely hot and dry. The fertile Central Valley supports major fruit, nut, vegetable, dairy, and wine production. Coastal ports and metropolitan areas connect California to Pacific trade, technology, entertainment, and manufacturing. Earthquakes, drought, wildfire, floods, and sea-level rise strongly shape settlement and planning.

Nauru.

Nauru is a tiny island country in the central Pacific Ocean, just south of the Equator. It has no land borders and covers only about twenty-one square kilometers, making it one of the world's smallest sovereign states. A coral reef surrounds the oval island. Most homes and roads occupy a narrow coastal plain, while a steep rise called Topside leads to a central limestone plateau. Buada Lagoon is the only notable inland body of water, and there are no permanent rivers.

The climate is hot, humid, and tropical throughout the year. Rainfall is highly variable, with droughts linked to changes in the Pacific Ocean. Limited groundwater, a small catchment area, and saltwater intrusion make rain collection and desalination important. Rich phosphate deposits formed from seabird droppings, but decades of mining stripped much of the plateau into jagged limestone pinnacles and left little land suitable for farming. Because the interior is difficult to use, nearly all settlement, the airport, and government offices cluster around the coast, especially near Yaren. Food and fuel are largely imported. Remaining phosphate, fishing rights, and services provide income, while land rehabilitation and climate resilience are long-term challenges.
