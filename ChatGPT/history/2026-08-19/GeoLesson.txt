Ohio.

Ohio lies in the east-central United States, where the Great Lakes region meets the Appalachian uplands. It borders Michigan, Pennsylvania, West Virginia, Kentucky, and Indiana. Across Lake Erie, it also faces the Canadian province of Ontario. Lake Erie shapes the state's northern edge, while the Ohio River forms much of its southern and southeastern boundary.

Glaciers flattened and enriched much of western and central Ohio, leaving broad till plains and productive soils that support corn, soybeans, and livestock. The eastern third rises into the dissected Appalachian Plateau, a landscape of wooded ridges, steep valleys, and historic coal country. Campbell Hill, in the west-central Bellefontaine Upland, is the state's highest point at one thousand five hundred forty-nine feet.

Ohio's drainage divides two enormous watersheds. The Maumee, Sandusky, Cuyahoga, and Grand rivers flow north into Lake Erie and ultimately the Atlantic by way of the Great Lakes and Saint Lawrence River. The Scioto, Muskingum, and Miami systems flow south to the Ohio River and then the Mississippi. This water access helped Cleveland, Toledo, Cincinnati, and other cities become manufacturing and transportation centers. Ohio has a humid continental climate, with warm summers, cold winters, lake-effect snow in the northeast, and frequent changes as air masses cross the interior.

Germany.

Germany occupies a central position in Europe and borders nine countries: Denmark, Poland, the Czech Republic, Austria, Switzerland, France, Luxembourg, Belgium, and the Netherlands. Its northern shore reaches both the North Sea and the Baltic Sea, giving ports such as Hamburg access to global trade.

The landscape descends in broad steps from south to north. The Bavarian Alps line the southern frontier, where the Zugspitze rises to nine thousand seven hundred twenty-one feet. North of the Alps are rolling foothills and lakes shaped by glaciers. Central Germany contains forested uplands and low mountain ranges, while the North German Plain opens toward the coasts with farmland, wetlands, and broad river valleys.

The Rhine is Germany's great western commercial artery, linking industrial regions and cities to the North Sea. The Elbe crosses the east and reaches the sea near Hamburg, while the Danube begins in southwestern Germany and flows east toward the Black Sea. The Main and the Rhine-Main-Danube Canal connect these drainage systems. Germany's temperate climate is moderated by Atlantic air in the west, with cooler winters and greater seasonal contrast toward the east and in the mountains. Rivers, navigable lowlands, central location, and nearby coalfields helped create a dense network of cities, factories, farms, railways, and inland ports.
