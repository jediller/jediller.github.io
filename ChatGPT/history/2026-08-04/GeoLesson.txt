Today's state is Texas.

Texas occupies the south-central United States. It borders New Mexico, Oklahoma, Arkansas, and Louisiana, while the Rio Grande forms much of its international boundary with Mexico. Its southeastern coast faces the Gulf of Mexico. The landscape steps upward from low coastal plains through rolling Hill Country and central prairies to the dry High Plains and the rugged mountains of far west Texas. The Rio Grande, Red, Brazos, and Colorado rivers cross or define the state, and Guadalupe Peak is its highest point.

Climate varies widely. East Texas is humid and forested, the Gulf Coast is hot and hurricane-prone, the Panhandle has colder winters and severe storms, and the west is desert or semiarid. Water availability helped concentrate cities and farms in the east and along river corridors, while the west remains more sparsely settled. Cattle, cotton, petroleum, Gulf ports, manufacturing, technology, and wind power all reflect this geographic range. Houston grew around a ship channel near the Gulf, Dallas and Fort Worth became an inland transport hub, and Austin developed along the Colorado River at the edge of Hill Country. Rapid growth also increases pressure on water supplies and exposes more people to floods, droughts, heat, and coastal storms.

Today's country is Malawi.

Malawi is a long, narrow, landlocked country in southeastern Africa. Zambia lies to the west, Tanzania to the northeast, and Mozambique wraps around much of the east, south, and southwest. The Great Rift Valley shapes the country. Lake Malawi fills much of its eastern side, and the Shire River carries the lake's water south toward the Zambezi. Highlands rise on both sides of the rift, including the Nyika Plateau in the north and Mount Mulanje in the south, the country's highest summit.

Malawi has a tropical wet-and-dry climate, with most rain falling from roughly November through April and a cooler dry season from May through August. Many people live along the lakeshore, around the Shire Valley, and in fertile highland districts where water and cultivable land are more available. Lake Malawi supports fishing, transport, and tourism, while the Shire River supplies much of the country's hydroelectric power. Agriculture employs a large share of the population; maize is the main food crop, and tobacco, tea, and sugar are important commercial crops. Because Malawi has no seacoast, trade depends on road and rail corridors through neighboring countries to ports in Mozambique, making regional transport links especially important.
