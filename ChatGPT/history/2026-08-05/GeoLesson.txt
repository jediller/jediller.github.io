Today's state is Maryland.

Maryland is a Mid-Atlantic state whose unusual outline stretches from the Atlantic coast and Chesapeake Bay westward into the Appalachian Mountains. It borders Pennsylvania to the north, Delaware and the Atlantic Ocean to the east, and Virginia, West Virginia, and the District of Columbia to the south and west. The Chesapeake Bay nearly divides the state into the Eastern Shore and Western Shore and gives Maryland an exceptionally long tidal shoreline.

The land rises in stages from east to west. The low Atlantic Coastal Plain contains beaches, marshes, the Eastern Shore, and both sides of the bay. Rolling Piedmont uplands cross central Maryland, followed by the Blue Ridge, Ridge and Valley, and Appalachian Plateau provinces. Backbone Mountain in the far west is the state's highest point at three thousand three hundred sixty feet. The Potomac forms much of the southern boundary, the Susquehanna enters the head of the Chesapeake, and the Patapsco reaches the bay at Baltimore.

Water shaped settlement and work. Baltimore developed as a major port, fertile lowlands support farming, and the bay supports fishing and recreation. The central corridor between Baltimore and Washington is densely populated, while western mountains have smaller communities, forests, farms, and outdoor tourism.

Today's country is Ghana.

Ghana lies in West Africa on the Gulf of Guinea, a part of the Atlantic Ocean. Côte d'Ivoire is to the west, Burkina Faso to the north, and Togo to the east. Its coastline is mostly low and sandy, backed by plains, lagoons, and river mouths. Inland, forested uplands cross the southwest and center, broad savanna plains cover much of the north, and the Akwapim-Togo ranges rise near the eastern border.

The Volta River system dominates Ghana's drainage. The Akosombo Dam created Lake Volta, one of the world's largest artificial lakes by surface area. The lake and river support hydroelectric power, fishing, irrigation, and transport, although their creation also transformed communities and landscapes. Smaller southern rivers, including the Pra, Ankobra, and Tano, flow toward the Gulf of Guinea.

Ghana is tropical, but rainfall varies greatly. The southwest is wetter and supports dense forest and cocoa growing, while northern Ghana has a longer dry season and savanna suited to grains and livestock. Dry, dusty harmattan winds can arrive from the Sahara. Accra and the port of Tema grew on the coast, Kumasi became a major inland center in the forest zone, and many rural livelihoods remain closely tied to seasonal rain, soils, and river water.
