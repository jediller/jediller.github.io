Today's state is Wisconsin.

Wisconsin lies in the Upper Midwest and touches two Great Lakes: Lake Superior in the north and Lake Michigan in the east. It borders Michigan, Minnesota, Iowa, and Illinois. The Mississippi and Saint Croix rivers form much of its western boundary, while the Wisconsin and Fox rivers cross the interior. Ice sheets shaped most of the state, leaving rolling plains, thousands of lakes, wetlands, and fertile soils. Southwestern Wisconsin escaped the last glaciation and remains the rugged Driftless Area, with steep ridges and deeply cut valleys.

Wisconsin has a humid continental climate with cold, snowy winters and warm summers. The Great Lakes moderate nearby temperatures and can add lake-effect snow. Glacial soils, dependable water, and grass-friendly summers helped dairy farming become central to the state's identity; corn, soybeans, cranberries, and potatoes are also important. Forests and lakes support timber, paper making, tourism, and recreation in the north. Milwaukee grew as a Lake Michigan port and manufacturing center, while the Fox River valley developed paper and food-processing industries. Great Lakes shipping and the Mississippi connect Wisconsin to distant markets, but winter cold, floods, and shoreline erosion remain practical geographic challenges.

Today's country is Libya.

Libya occupies a broad stretch of North Africa on the Mediterranean Sea. It borders Tunisia, Algeria, Niger, Chad, Sudan, and Egypt. The Sahara covers most of its territory, including gravel plains, sand seas, rocky plateaus, and isolated oases. Narrower coastal lowlands hold most farmland and cities. Higher ground includes the Nafusa Mountains in the northwest and the greener Jebel Akhdar in the northeast. Libya has no permanent rivers; short wadis flow only after rain. The Great Man-Made River system carries ancient groundwater from deep desert aquifers toward the coast.

The interior is extremely hot and dry, while the coast has milder, wetter Mediterranean winters and hot summers. Water scarcity and harsh desert terrain concentrate most residents in the coastal corridor, especially around Tripoli, Misrata, and Benghazi. Oil and natural-gas fields, many in and around the Sirte Basin, dominate exports and connect inland production sites with coastal terminals. Agriculture is limited mainly to irrigated oases and coastal areas, where olives, dates, grains, and vegetables can grow. Libya's long Mediterranean frontage gives it strategic ports and links to Europe, but sparse rainfall, groundwater depletion, dust storms, and great distances make settlement, transport, and economic diversification difficult.
