United States State: Michigan

Michigan occupies two peninsulas in the upper Midwest, separated by the Straits of Mackinac. It borders Wisconsin, Indiana, and Ohio on land and shares water boundaries with Illinois, Minnesota, and Canada. Four Great Lakes, Superior, Michigan, Huron, and Erie, surround the state, giving it more than three thousand miles of Great Lakes shoreline. Ice-age glaciers shaped its inland lakes, sandy ridges, fertile southern plains, and northern forests. The western Upper Peninsula is hillier and contains ancient rock rich in iron and copper, while the Lower Peninsula is generally lower and more gently rolling. Lake-effect snow makes winters especially snowy along western and northern shores, while the lakes moderate temperatures near the coast. Most residents live in the southern Lower Peninsula, where flatter land, transport links, and access to Detroit and the Great Lakes supported cities, automobile manufacturing, and trade. The north supports forestry, mining, tourism, and outdoor recreation. Fruit farms thrive near the milder Lake Michigan shore, and Great Lakes water remains central to industry, shipping, fishing, and daily life.

Sovereign Country: Japan

Japan is an island country off the eastern coast of Asia, between the Pacific Ocean and the Sea of Japan. Its long northeast-to-southwest archipelago includes the main islands of Hokkaido, Honshu, Shikoku, Kyushu, and Okinawa, plus thousands of smaller islands. Mountains and hills cover about three-quarters of the land, leaving narrow coastal plains where most people, farms, cities, and transport routes are concentrated. Mount Fuji is the highest peak. Short, steep rivers descend quickly from the mountains and supply water and hydroelectric power, but can flood after heavy rain. Japan lies where tectonic plates meet, so earthquakes, volcanoes, and tsunamis are persistent hazards. Climate varies from snowy, cool Hokkaido to subtropical Okinawa; monsoons, typhoons, and strong seasonal contrasts affect agriculture and settlement. Tokyo and other major cities grew on broad coastal plains beside deep ports. Limited flat land encourages dense development and extensive rail networks, while surrounding seas support fishing and international commerce. Mountain forests help protect watersheds, but steep slopes and crowded coasts make disaster planning essential.
