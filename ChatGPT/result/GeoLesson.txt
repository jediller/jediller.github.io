Today's state is Wyoming.

Wyoming lies in the Mountain West and meets Montana to the north, South Dakota and Nebraska to the east, Colorado and Utah to the south, and Idaho to the west. The Continental Divide crosses the state through a landscape of high plains, broad basins, and rugged ranges. The Wind River Range contains Gannett Peak, Wyoming's highest point at about 13,810 feet. The Teton Range rises sharply above Jackson Hole, while Yellowstone's plateau holds geysers, hot springs, and the headwaters of major rivers. Water flows toward several distant seas: the Snake River joins the Columbia system, the Yellowstone and North Platte join the Missouri system, and the Green River feeds the Colorado. Much of Wyoming is high, dry, windy, and sparsely populated, with cold winters and short growing seasons. Towns developed along river valleys, rail routes, mining areas, and mountain gateways. Open grasslands support cattle and sheep, while coal, oil, natural gas, trona, wind power, and tourism remain important. Limited water and long distances strongly shape settlement and economic life.

Today's country is Latvia.

Latvia is a Baltic country in northeastern Europe. It borders Estonia to the north, Russia to the east, Belarus to the southeast, Lithuania to the south, and the Baltic Sea and Gulf of Riga to the west. Most of the land is a low, gently rolling plain shaped by glaciers, with extensive forests, wetlands, sandy soils, and thousands of lakes. Gaiziņkalns, the highest point, rises only about 312 meters, or 1,024 feet. The Daugava River crosses the country from the southeast to the Gulf of Riga and has long served as a trade route and source of hydroelectric power. The Gauja, Lielupe, and Venta are other important rivers. Latvia has cool summers, cold winters, and a climate moderated near the coast by the Baltic Sea. Riga, the capital and largest city, grew beside the Daugava near an ice-free port and remains the country's main population and transport center. Forest resources support timber and paper industries, while fertile lowlands support grains, potatoes, dairy farming, and livestock. Ports connect Latvia with Baltic trade, and its flat terrain has encouraged roads, railways, and settlement around river valleys.
