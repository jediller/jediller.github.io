Today’s state is New York.

New York lies in the northeastern United States. Pennsylvania and New Jersey border it on the south, while Connecticut, Massachusetts, and Vermont lie to the east. Canada forms much of the northern boundary. Lake Erie and Lake Ontario edge western New York, and the Niagara River connects them at Niagara Falls. The Saint Lawrence River carries water from Lake Ontario toward the Atlantic. The Hudson River runs south through eastern New York to New York Harbor, while the Mohawk River cuts an important east-west passage through the interior.

The Adirondack Mountains dominate the north, the Catskills rise southwest of Albany, and the Finger Lakes occupy glacier-carved valleys in the west-central region. Long Island reaches east into the Atlantic beside New York City. These waterways and lowland corridors shaped settlement and trade. The Erie Canal linked the Great Lakes to the Hudson River and helped New York City become the nation’s largest port and urban center. Albany grew near the Hudson and Mohawk junction, while Buffalo and Rochester developed along Great Lakes and canal routes. Inland New York has cold winters, warm summers, and heavy lake-effect snow in some western and northern areas. The coast is milder but vulnerable to hurricanes and flooding. Fertile valleys support dairy farming, apples, grapes, and other crops, while forests, finance, manufacturing, tourism, and transportation reflect the state’s varied geography.

Today’s country is Costa Rica.

Costa Rica lies on the Central American isthmus between Nicaragua and Panama, with the Caribbean Sea to the east and the Pacific Ocean to the west. Volcanic mountain chains cross the interior, including the Guanacaste and Central ranges, while the Talamanca Range rises farther south. Cerro Chirripó is the country’s highest point. Short, steep rivers descend from the mountains to coastal lowlands. The Caribbean side is generally wetter, while the Pacific side has a more distinct dry season. Higher elevations in the Central Valley have a milder tropical climate.

Most Costa Ricans live in the Central Valley around San José, where moderate temperatures, volcanic soils, and transportation routes favored cities and coffee farming. Bananas and pineapples grow in warmer lowlands. Mountains provide water for hydroelectric power, but steep slopes and intense rain can cause landslides and flooding. Costa Rica lies near active tectonic boundaries, so earthquakes and volcanic eruptions remain hazards. Its position between two continents and two oceans supports exceptional biodiversity in rain forests, cloud forests, dry forests, mangroves, and coastal waters. Protected areas and ecotourism are central to the economy, alongside farming, manufacturing, and services. The country’s ports connect it to Caribbean and Pacific trade routes.
