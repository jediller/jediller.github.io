Minnesota.

Minnesota lies in the north-central United States, along the Canadian border. Lake Superior forms part of its northeast edge, while the Red River of the North marks much of the western boundary with North Dakota. Thousands of lakes occupy basins left by glaciers, and the Mississippi River begins at Lake Itasca before flowing through the Twin Cities. The north and east are largely forested and rocky, the west and south open into prairie and rich farmland, and the northeast contains the iron-rich Mesabi Range.

Minnesota has a humid continental climate, with cold winters, warm summers, and large seasonal temperature changes. The Mississippi and Minnesota rivers helped make Minneapolis and Saint Paul a transportation and industrial center. Duluth became a major inland port because Lake Superior connects through the Great Lakes to the Atlantic. Fertile southern and western soils support corn, soybeans, sugar beets, dairy farming, and livestock, while northern forests, lakes, and mineral deposits support forestry, mining, recreation, and tourism.

Burundi.

Burundi is a small, landlocked country in Africa's Great Lakes region. Rwanda lies to the north, Tanzania to the east and south, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo to the west across part of Lake Tanganyika. Much of the country is a high plateau crossed by hills and the Congo-Nile divide. Water on the eastern side enters the Ruvubu and Kagera river systems and ultimately the Nile basin, while western streams descend toward Lake Tanganyika and the Congo basin.

Elevation gives much of Burundi a milder tropical highland climate than its latitude suggests, though the low plain around Lake Tanganyika is hotter. Rainfall and fertile upland soils support dense rural settlement and crops including beans, bananas, cassava, maize, tea, and coffee. Steep cultivated slopes are vulnerable to erosion when vegetation is cleared. Gitega, the political capital, sits on the central plateau, while Bujumbura on Lake Tanganyika remains the largest city and principal commercial center. Because Burundi has no seacoast, trade depends on lake transport and long overland routes through neighboring countries.
