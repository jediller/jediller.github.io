In 1867, the United States formally took possession of the vast territory of Alaska from the Russian Empire following a formal transfer ceremony held at Sitka. Negotiated by United States Secretary of State William H. Seward for the purchase price of 7.2 million dollars, or roughly two cents per acre, the acquisition initially faced public skepticism and was derided by critics as Seward's Folly and Seward's Icebox. However, the acquisition added nearly six hundred thousand square miles to the American domain, securing an extraordinary wealth of natural resources, including petroleum, gold, timber, and world-class commercial fisheries, alongside vital strategic positioning in the North Pacific and Arctic. Today, the historic transfer is commemorated annually as Alaska Day.


In 1851, American novelist Herman Melville's epic literary masterpiece Moby-Dick was published for the first time by British publisher Richard Bentley in London under the initial title The Whale, appearing in a three-volume edition prior to its American publication in New York a month later. Chronicling the obsessive voyage of the whaling ship Pequod and its monomaniacal captain, Ahab, in relentless pursuit of the titular white whale, the novel wove complex allegorical themes of fate, human ambition, and the natural world with documentary details of nineteenth-century commercial whaling. Although met with mixed reviews during Melville's lifetime, Moby-Dick was rediscovered in the twentieth century and is now revered worldwide as one of the greatest works of American literature.


In 1967, the Soviet planetary probe Venera 4 successfully entered the dense, hostile atmosphere of Venus, achieving the first in situ scientific transmission from within the atmosphere of another planet. Deploying a parachute system and scientific sensor capsule as it descended through the cloud layers, Venera 4 transmitted critical data back to Earth for ninety-six minutes, revealing an atmosphere composed predominantly of carbon dioxide with extreme atmospheric pressure and scorching temperatures. The mission provided humanity's first direct atmospheric measurements of our planetary neighbor and laid the foundation for future robotic planetary exploration across the solar system.


In 1931, prolific American inventor and entrepreneur Thomas Alva Edison passed away at age eighty-four at his home in West Orange, New Jersey. Holding 1,093 United States patents, Edison pioneered transformative technologies that revolutionized modern civilization, including the practical incandescent electric light bulb, the phonograph, the motion picture camera, and the world's first industrial research laboratory at Menlo Park.
