Welcome to today's geography lesson exploring the United States state of Maine and the South Pacific nation of New Zealand.


Maine, celebrated as the Pine Tree State, is situated in the northeasternmost corner of the United States as the largest state in New England. Covering approximately thirty-five thousand square miles, Maine borders New Hampshire to the west, the Canadian provinces of Quebec and New Brunswick to the northwest and east, and the Atlantic Ocean to the south. Maine's physical geography is distinguished by its heavily indented, rocky drowned coastline, featuring over three thousand offshore islands, deep saltwater bays, and iconic granite headlands exemplified by Acadia National Park on Mount Desert Island. Inland, the terrain rises into the heavily forested Appalachian Mountains, crowned by Mount Katahdin at five thousand two hundred and sixty-seven feet, which marks the dramatic northern terminus of the Appalachian Trail. Over eighty-nine percent of Maine is covered by dense mixed forests known as the Great North Woods, drained by major waterways including the Kennebec, Penobscot, and Saint John Rivers. Economically, Maine's geography underpins the nation's premier lobster and cold-water seafood harvest, extensive commercial timber harvesting and specialty papermaking, wild lowbush blueberry agriculture, and a vibrant four-season outdoor tourism industry.


New Zealand, known in Maori as Aotearoa, is an island nation situated in the southwestern Pacific Ocean roughly one thousand miles southeast of Australia. Comprising two primary landmasses, the North Island and the South Island, alongside numerous smaller islands, New Zealand spans approximately one hundred and three thousand square miles along the active convergent boundary of the Pacific and Indo-Australian tectonic plates. The North Island features active volcanic plateaus, geothermal geyser fields around Rotorua, and Lake Taupo, the nation's largest lake, formed within an ancient volcanic caldera. In contrast, the larger South Island is dominated along its spine by the soaring Southern Alps, crowned by Aoraki Mount Cook at twelve thousand two hundred and eighteen feet, featuring dramatic glaciated alpine valleys, turquoise glacial lakes, and world-renowned fjords including Milford Sound. To the east of the mountains lie the fertile Canterbury Plains. Isolated for eighty million years, New Zealand evolved extraordinary endemic wildlife, including flightless kiwi birds. Economically, New Zealand leverages its temperate maritime climate and abundant rainfall to lead the world in dairy and wool exports, high-value wine viticulture, kiwi fruit production, and sustainable geothermal and hydroelectric power generation.
