In 1904, the New York City Subway officially opened its first underground rapid transit line, operated by the Interborough Rapid Transit Company. Spanning nine point one miles from City Hall station in Lower Manhattan to One Hundred and Forty-Fifth Street in Harlem, the line served twenty-eight stations. Over one hundred thousand passengers paid a five-cent fare to ride the underground electric trains on opening day. The engineering marvel established a rapid transportation backbone that reshaped urban mobility, facilitated residential expansion, and grew into one of the world's most extensive subway networks.

In 1787, the first essay of the Federalist Papers, Federalist Number One, was published in The Independent Journal in New York under the pseudonym Publius. Authored by Alexander Hamilton, the introductory essay urged citizens to ratify the newly drafted United States Constitution, framing the debate as a test of whether societies can establish good government through reflection and choice rather than accident and force. The eighty-five essays written by Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay remain the foundational commentary on American constitutional governance.
In 1938, DuPont chemical corporation formally announced the invention of the world's first fully synthetic polymer fiber under the trade name Nylon at the New York World's Fair preview. Developed by research chemist Wallace Carothers, the revolutionary polyamide material exhibited unprecedented tensile strength, elasticity, and durability. Initially used in synthetic bristle toothbrushes and women's hosiery before becoming vital for military parachutes during the Second World War, nylon inaugurated the modern synthetic materials era.
