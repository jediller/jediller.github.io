In 1867, world-renowned physicist and chemist Marie Sklodowska Curie was born in Warsaw, Poland. Emigrating to France to pursue higher education at the Sorbonne in Paris, Curie conducted foundational scientific research on the phenomenon of radioactivity, a term she coined. Alongside her husband Pierre Curie, she discovered the radioactive elements polonium, which she named after her native Poland, and radium. Curie became the first woman to win a Nobel Prize when she shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics, and in 1911 she made history as the first person to win a second Nobel Prize, awarded in Chemistry for her isolation of pure radium. To this day, she remains the only individual ever honored with Nobel Prizes in two distinct scientific fields, fundamentally shaping modern physics, oncology, and nuclear chemistry.
In 1916, social worker and suffragist Jeannette Rankin of Montana was elected to the United States House of Representatives, making history as the first woman ever elected to the United States Congress. Taking her seat in the Sixty-Fifth Congress four full years before the ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment granted women universal voting rights nationwide, Rankin declared: "I may be the first woman member of Congress, but I won't be the last." During her historic tenure, she championed women's voting rights and labor welfare, helping open the doors of American federal legislative governance to future generations of women leaders.
In 1940, the original Tacoma Narrows Bridge, a suspension bridge spanning the Puget Sound in Washington State nicknamed Galloping Gertie due to its pronounced vertical oscillations, collapsed during forty-two-mile-per-hour gale-force winds. The spectacular aerodynamic failure, captured on historic motion picture film as the center span twisted violently before falling into the waters below, revolutionized civil engineering and bridge design, leading to the integration of comprehensive aerodynamic modeling and wind-tunnel testing in all modern suspension bridge architecture.
In 1910, the Wright brothers and department store entrepreneur Max Morehouse completed the world's first commercial air cargo flight, flying sixty-five miles from Dayton to Columbus, Ohio, aboard a Wright Model B biplane carrying bolts of commercial silk, establishing the foundation of the modern commercial air freight industry.
