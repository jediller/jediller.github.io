In 1517, German theologian and Augustinian monk Martin Luther famously posted his Ninety-Five Theses on the wooden doors of All Saints' Church in Wittenberg, Germany, igniting the Protestant Reformation. Written in Latin as a formal scholarly invitation for academic disputation, Luther's Ninety-Five Theses sharply criticized the church's commercial sale of plenary indulgences, arguing that salvation was granted solely by divine grace and faith in Jesus Christ rather than through financial transactions or ecclesiastical dispensations. Rapidly translated into German and widely disseminated across Europe via the newly invented movable-type printing press, Luther's bold theological challenge fractured Western Christendom, fundamentally transforming European political structures, religious philosophy, literacy, and individual conscience, and setting the course for the modern era.
In 1864, Nevada was officially admitted to the Union as the thirty-sixth United States state during the height of the American Civil War. Telegraphed to Washington, D.C. in its entirety in the longest Morse code dispatch ever transmitted, the Nevada state constitution was rapidly ratified to ensure President Abraham Lincoln had additional electoral support and congressional votes for the passage of the Thirteenth Amendment abolishing slavery. Nicknamed the Silver State and Battle Born, Nevada's statehood secured vital Comstock Lode silver and gold reserves that stabilized the Union Treasury during the final, decisive months of the Civil War.
In 1941, fourteen years of continuous artistic and engineering labor concluded as the colossal Mount Rushmore National Memorial was officially completed in the Black Hills near Keystone, South Dakota. Conceived by state historian Doane Robinson and executed by master sculptor Gutzon Borglum and his son Lincoln alongside four hundred skilled miners and stonecutters, the monumental sixty-foot-tall granite sculptures depict Presidents George Washington, Thomas Jefferson, Theodore Roosevelt, and Abraham Lincoln, symbolizing the birth, growth, development, and preservation of the United States.
In 2000, Expedition One launched from the Baikonur Cosmodrome in Kazakhstan aboard a Russian Soyuz rocket, carrying American commander William Shepherd and Russian cosmonauts Yuri Gidzenko and Sergei Krikalev to dock with the infant International Space Station, inaugurating an unbroken era of continuous human habitation in low Earth orbit that has persisted to this day.
