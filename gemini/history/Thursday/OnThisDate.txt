On This Date: November 26.
On November 26, 1789, the United States observed its first national Thanksgiving Day following a proclamation issued by President George Washington. Responding to a resolution passed by Congress just months after the ratification of the U.S. Constitution, Washington designated the last Thursday of November as a day of public thanksgiving and prayer. He urged Americans to express gratitude for the peaceful establishment of the new constitutional government, national independence, and civil and religious liberties. While earlier colonial observances had been held at regional or state levels, Washington's proclamation set the precedent for a unified national celebration that later became a permanent federal holiday.
On November 26, 1922, British archaeologist Howard Carter and his patron, George Herbert, the fifth Earl of Carnarvon, entered the burial chamber of Pharaoh Tutankhamun in Egypt's Valley of the Kings. After years of fruitless excavation, Carter's team had uncovered steps leading to a sealed doorway earlier that month. On this day, Carter made a small breach in the upper left corner of the barrier, inserted a candle to test for foul gases, and peered inside. When Carnarvon asked if he could see anything, Carter famously replied that he saw wonderful things. Unlike most royal tombs, Tutankhamun's resting place was remarkably intact, yielding thousands of priceless artifacts and sparking a global fascination with ancient Egypt.
On November 26, 1942, the romantic drama Casablanca premiered at the Hollywood Theater in New York City, just weeks after Allied forces landed in French North Africa during World War Two. Directed by Michael Curtiz and starring Humphrey Bogart and Ingrid Bergman, the film is set in the Moroccan city of Casablanca and follows an American expatriate nightclub owner forced to choose between his love for a former flame and helping her Resistance-leader husband escape Nazi-occupied Europe. Initially conceived as a standard studio production, Casablanca became a cultural phenomenon, winning the Academy Award for Best Picture and cementing its place among the most celebrated films in cinema history.
On November 26, 2003, the supersonic passenger airliner Concorde made its final flight, landing at Filton Airfield in Bristol, England, and bringing an end to twenty-seven years of commercial supersonic travel. Jointly developed by British and French aerospace engineers, Concorde routinely crossed the Atlantic Ocean at more than twice the speed of sound, cutting the journey between London or Paris and New York to roughly three and a half hours. Although the aircraft was an engineering marvel, rising maintenance costs, a downturn in air travel, and the aftermath of a fatal crash in 2000 led British Airways and Air France to retire the fleet in 2003.
