Welcome to today's geography lesson exploring the United States state of Maine and the East Asian island nation of Japan.
Maine, known as the Pine Tree State, sits in the far northeastern corner of the United States as the largest of the six New England states, covering roughly thirty-five thousand square miles. Bordered by New Hampshire to the west, the Canadian provinces of Quebec and New Brunswick to the north and east, and the Gulf of Maine on the Atlantic Ocean to the south, Maine boasts a deeply indented, rocky coastline stretching nearly three thousand five hundred miles when counting its bays, peninsulas, and thousands of coastal islands. Moving inland from the rugged coast and Acadia National Park, the landscape rises into rolling uplands, glacial lakes such as Moosehead Lake, and the northern terminus of the Appalachian Mountains, crowned by Mount Katahdin at five thousand two hundred and sixty-nine feet. Dense boreal and mixed forests cover nearly ninety percent of the state, drained by the Kennebec, Penobscot, and Androscoggin rivers. Maine's cold, nutrient-rich coastal waters and vast timberlands shape an economy rooted in commercial lobster and scallop fishing, forestry and paper manufacturing, shipbuilding along Bath and Portland, wild blueberry agriculture, and four-season tourism.
Japan is an elongated volcanic archipelago stretching nearly two thousand miles along the Pacific Ring of Fire off the eastern coast of Asia, separated from the Korean Peninsula and mainland China by the Sea of Japan and facing the Pacific Ocean to the east. Spanning roughly one hundred and forty-six thousand square miles across more than fourteen thousand islands, Japan's population and economy center on four main islands: Hokkaido, Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu. Roughly three-quarters of the country is steep, forested mountainous terrain, highlighted by the Japanese Alps and iconic Mount Fuji rising to twelve thousand three hundred and eighty-nine feet on Honshu. Because flat coastal plains such as the Kanto Plain around Tokyo and the Kansai Plain around Osaka are scarce, Japan's cities, high-speed rail corridors, and terraced rice farms cluster tightly along narrow river valleys and sheltered bays. Frequent earthquakes, volcanic activity, and seasonal typhoons have shaped Japan's world-leading civil engineering, while its maritime location supports global leadership in automotive manufacturing, robotics, precision electronics, fisheries, and international trade.
