On This Date: November 27.
On November 27, 1895, Swedish chemist, engineer, and inventor Alfred Nobel signed his last will and testament at the Swedish-Norwegian Club in Paris, France, establishing the Nobel Prizes. Having amassed an immense fortune from his invention of dynamite and other industrial explosives, Nobel directed that the bulk of his estate be placed in a trust to fund annual international awards for individuals who conferred the greatest benefit to humankind. His will established five original categories: physics, chemistry, medicine, literature, and peace. Awarded for the first time in 1901, the Nobel Prizes remain among the world's most prestigious honors for scientific discovery, literary achievement, and humanitarian diplomacy.
On November 27, 1978, San Francisco Mayor George Moscone and City Supervisor Harvey Milk were assassinated inside San Francisco City Hall by former supervisor Dan White. Milk, who had won election in 1977, was the first openly gay person elected to public office in California and a trailblazing advocate for civil rights and neighborhood equality, while Moscone was a progressive mayor known for building diverse political coalitions. Their deaths shocked the nation, sparked candlelight vigils across San Francisco, and cemented Milk's legacy as an enduring icon of the modern LGBTQ civil rights movement.
On November 27, 1989, surgeons at the University of Chicago Medical Center performed the world's first successful living-donor liver transplant. Twenty-one-month-old Alyssa Smith, who suffered from a life-threatening congenital liver condition, received a portion of her mother Teresa Smith's liver during a groundbreaking operation led by surgical team chief Christoph Broelsch alongside Richard Thistlethwaite, Thomas Heffron, and Jean Emond. Because the human liver is capable of regenerating both in the donor and the recipient, the pioneering procedure opened a vital new pathway for saving pediatric and adult patients facing critical organ shortages.
On November 27, 2001, astronomers using NASA's Hubble Space Telescope announced the first direct detection of an atmosphere around a planet orbiting a star outside our Solar System. Observing the gas giant exoplanet HD 209458 b, nicknamed Osiris, as it passed in front of its parent star roughly one hundred and fifty light-years away, researchers detected the distinct chemical signature of sodium in the planet's superheated upper atmosphere. Classified as a hot Jupiter because it orbits extremely close to its star, Osiris marked a watershed moment in exoplanet science, demonstrating that telescopes could analyze the atmospheric composition of distant worlds.
