In 1886, the Statue of Liberty, formally titled Liberty Enlightening the World, was officially dedicated in New York Harbor by President Grover Cleveland. Designed by French sculptor Frederic-Auguste Bartholdi with an internal structural iron framework engineered by Gustave Eiffel, the colossal copper neoclassical statue was presented as a gift from the people of France to the United States commemorating the centennial of American independence and the enduring alliance between the two republics. Standing atop a monumental granite pedestal on Bedloe's Island, the three-hundred-and-five-foot beacon welcomed millions of arriving immigrants and grew into the world's most recognizable symbol of freedom and democracy.
In 1962, the Cuban Missile Crisis came to a peaceful conclusion when Soviet Premier Nikita Khrushchev publicly announced that the Soviet Union would dismantle and withdraw all offensive nuclear missiles deployed in Cuba. The announcement followed twelve days of intense secret negotiations with President John F. Kennedy, who pledged that the United States would respect Cuban sovereignty and refrain from invading the island, while secretly agreeing to withdraw American Jupiter missiles stationed in Turkey. Khrushchev's decision defused the most dangerous nuclear standoff in human history, leading directly to the establishment of the Moscow-Washington direct communications hotline and the 1963 Nuclear Test Ban Treaty.
In 1965, the construction of the Gateway Arch in Saint Louis, Missouri was triumphantly completed as engineers hoisted and secured the final ten-ton triangular stainless steel keystone section into place atop the apex. Designed by visionary Finnish-American architect Eero Saarinen, the sixty-three-story, six-hundred-and-thirty-foot weighted catenary arch was constructed as a monument commemorating the westward expansion of the United States. Clad in gleaming stainless steel, the architectural marvel remains the tallest arch in the world and an enduring symbol of American architectural ambition.
In 1636, Harvard College, now Harvard University, was officially founded in Cambridge, Massachusetts by a vote of the Great and General Court of the Massachusetts Bay Colony. Established to educate puritan clergy and civic leaders, the institution was named two years later in honor of its first benefactor, young minister John Harvard, who bequeathed his four-hundred-volume library and half of his estate to the nascent academy. As the oldest institution of higher learning in the United States, Harvard played a seminal role in shaping American intellectual, legal, and political history.
