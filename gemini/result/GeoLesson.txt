Welcome to today's geography lesson exploring the northeastern United States state of Maine and the Iberian maritime nation of Portugal.
Maine, celebrated as the Pine Tree State, is situated in the New England region of the northeastern United States. Encompassing roughly thirty-five thousand square miles, Maine is bounded by New Hampshire to the west, the Atlantic Ocean to the south and east, and the Canadian provinces of Quebec and New Brunswick to the northwest and northeast. Over eighty-nine percent of the state is blanketed by dense boreal and northern hardwood forests, making it the most heavily forested state in the nation. In the interior, the rugged Longfellow Mountains, an extension of the Appalachian chain, rise to four thousand feet, crowned by Mount Katahdin at five thousand two hundred and sixty-eight feet, the northern terminus of the Appalachian Trail. Maine's Atlantic coastline boasts over three thousand miles of tidal shoreline featuring deep rocky fjords, dramatic granite headlands, and more than four thousand picturesque coastal islands, including Mount Desert Island, home to Acadia National Park. Economically, Maine leverages its geographic assets through sustainable forestry, paper manufacturing, naval shipbuilding along the Kennebec River, world-leading commercial lobster harvesting, and outdoor eco-tourism.
Portugal, officially the Portuguese Republic, occupies the southwestern edge of the Iberian Peninsula in Southwestern Europe, facing the Atlantic Ocean. Covering approximately thirty-five thousand square miles, Portugal shares its eastern and northern land border with Spain, while its western and southern boundaries comprise nearly one thousand one hundred miles of rugged Atlantic coastline, complemented by the volcanic archipelagos of the Azores and Madeira. Topographically, the country is bisected by the Tagus River, which flows westward through Lisbon into the Atlantic. North of the Tagus, the landscape is mountainous and rainy, dominated by the granite peaks of the Serra da Estrela, rising to nearly six thousand five hundred feet, and the terraced schist valleys of the Douro River, where ancient vineyards produce world-famous port wine. South of the Tagus, the terrain softens into the rolling golden plains of the Alentejo, blanketed with cork oak forests and olive groves, before meeting the sandstone cliffs of the Algarve. Strategically, Portugal's Atlantic geography positioned it as the historical birthplace of the Age of Discovery, and today the nation serves as a vital maritime crossroads, leading Europe in renewable wind and solar power integration and sustainable cork manufacturing.
