Welcome to today's geography lesson exploring the Pacific Northwest United States state of Washington and the North Atlantic island nation of Iceland.
Washington, celebrated as the Evergreen State, is situated in the Pacific Northwest region of the United States. Spanning roughly seventy-one thousand square miles, Washington is bordered by the Canadian province of British Columbia to the north, Idaho to the east, Oregon to the south along the Columbia River, and the Pacific Ocean to the west. The state exhibits remarkable geographic contrast divided by the Cascade Mountain Range. Western Washington is defined by the deep saltwater fjord system of Puget Sound, home to major urban centers in Seattle and Tacoma, alongside the lush temperate rainforests of the Olympic Peninsula and majestic volcanic peaks including Mount Rainier, rising to fourteen thousand four hundred and eleven feet, Mount Adams, and Mount St. Helens. In contrast, Eastern Washington lies in the rain shadow of the Cascades, characterized by the semi-arid grasslands, basalt plateaus, and deeply carved coulees of the Columbia River Basin. Economically, Washington capitalizes on its geography through commercial aerospace manufacturing, global software and cloud technology, maritime shipping, and world-leading agriculture, producing the nation's premier harvests of apples, sweet cherries, and hops, supported by extensive hydroelectric power from the Grand Coulee Dam.
Iceland, an island nation in the North Atlantic Ocean just south of the Arctic Circle, encompasses approximately forty thousand square miles along the divergent tectonic boundary of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Positioned over a volcanic hotspot where the North American and Eurasian tectonic plates pull apart, Iceland is one of the most volcanically dynamic places on Earth. Its landscape is an awe-inspiring juxtaposition of fire and ice, featuring active volcanic fissures, vast lava fields, natural hot springs, and monumental glaciers. Vatnajokull, the largest glacier in Europe by volume, covers over three thousand square miles, while dramatic glaciated fjords carve deeply into the northern and eastern coastlines. Due to the moderating influence of the warm North Atlantic Current, Iceland's coastal climate remains surprisingly temperate given its subarctic latitude. Strategically located at a vital North Atlantic maritime crossroads, Iceland has transformed its unique geography into immense sustainable wealth, generating nearly one hundred percent of its electricity and domestic heating from clean geothermal and hydroelectric sources, supporting world-leading marine fisheries, aluminum smelting, and thriving international eco-tourism.
