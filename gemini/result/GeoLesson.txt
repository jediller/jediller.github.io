Welcome to today's geography lesson exploring the United States state of Idaho and the Southwestern Pacific island nation of New Zealand.


Idaho, known as the Gem State, is located in the Pacific Northwest and Rocky Mountain regions of the United States. Spanning roughly eighty-three thousand five hundred square miles, Idaho ranks as the fourteenth-largest state by land area. It borders Washington and Oregon to the west, Nevada and Utah to the south, Montana and Wyoming to the east, and British Columbia to the north. Idaho's physical geography is dominated by rugged wilderness and dramatic elevation changes, defined by the towering peaks of the Rocky Mountains, including the Sawtooth, Bitterroot, and Salmon River ranges. Central Idaho is carved by the Salmon River, the River of No Return, flowing through deep wilderness canyons, while along the western border lies Hells Canyon, the deepest river gorge in North America. In southern Idaho, the crescent-shaped Snake River Plain features fertile volcanic soils and majestic waterfalls such as Shoshone Falls. Economically, Idaho is a powerhouse in agricultural production, leading the nation in potatoes, alongside wheat, barley, sugar beets, and commercial trout farming in the Hagerman Valley. Boise, the state capital, anchors major semiconductor manufacturing and software design, while vast forestlands support a thriving timber and outdoor tourism industry.


New Zealand, known in Maori as Aotearoa, is an island nation located in the southwestern Pacific Ocean, approximately one thousand miles southeast of Australia across the Tasman Sea. Spanning roughly one hundred and three thousand square miles, New Zealand consists of two main landmasses—the North Island and the South Island—along with smaller offshore islands. Positioned along the active boundary of the Pacific and Indo-Australian tectonic plates, New Zealand possesses exceptional geological variety. The North Island is characterized by volcanic activity, featuring the central volcanic plateau with active volcanoes Mount Ruapehu and Mount Ngauruhoe, Lake Taupo, and the geothermal geysers of Rotorua. In contrast, the South Island is dominated by the Southern Alps, crowned by Mount Cook, or Aoraki, at twelve thousand two hundred and eighteen feet, descending into massive glaciers and the dramatic fjords of Fiordland, including Milford Sound. Economically, New Zealand supports premier exports of dairy, wool, lamb, kiwi, and wine, powered by abundant geothermal and hydroelectric energy, alongside world-renowned ecotourism and film production hubs centered around Auckland and the capital of Wellington.
