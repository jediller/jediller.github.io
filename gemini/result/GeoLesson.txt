Welcome to today's geography lesson exploring the United States state of Tennessee and the South American nation of Colombia.
Tennessee, known as the Volunteer State, is a landlocked state in the Southeastern United States bordered by eight states, including Kentucky and Virginia to the north, North Carolina to the east, Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi to the south, and Arkansas and Missouri along the Mississippi River to the west. Covering roughly forty-two thousand one hundred square miles, Tennessee is traditionally divided into three Grand Divisions reflected on its state flag: East, Middle, and West Tennessee. East Tennessee is dominated by the Blue Ridge and Great Smoky Mountains along the North Carolina border, rising to Kuwohi at six thousand six hundred and forty-three feet, alongside the Tennessee River valley. Middle Tennessee centers on the fertile limestone Nashville Basin and the surrounding Highland Rim, home to the capital city of Nashville. West Tennessee slopes gently from the Tennessee River toward the Mississippi River alluvial plain and the river port city of Memphis. Tennessee's humid subtropical climate and central river networks support a diverse economy anchored by automotive manufacturing, healthcare management, global air freight logistics, agriculture, and country and blues music tourism.
Colombia, officially the Republic of Colombia, sits at the northwestern gateway of South America, uniquely bordering both the Pacific Ocean to the west and the Caribbean Sea to the north, while sharing land borders with Panama, Venezuela, Brazil, Peru, and Ecuador. Spanning roughly four hundred and forty thousand square miles, Colombia is defined by three dramatic branches of the Andes Mountains, known as the Western, Central, and Eastern Cordilleras, which fan northward across the country and house high-altitude plateaus and valleys where major cities such as the capital Bogota and Medellin are located. Beyond the Andean spine, Colombia's geography encompasses humid Pacific and Caribbean coastal lowlands, the vast eastern Orinoco savannas called Los Llanos, and the dense Amazon rainforest in the south. Because Colombia straddles the equator, temperatures vary primarily by elevation rather than season, creating distinct ecological zones from tropical beaches to snow-capped peaks. This geographic variety underpins a major economy built on petroleum, coal, emeralds, cut flowers, specialty coffee, bananas, and expanding technology and manufacturing hubs.
