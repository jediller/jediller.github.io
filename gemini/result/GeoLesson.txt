Welcome to today's geography lesson exploring the United States state of Vermont and the East African nation of Kenya.
Vermont, affectionately known as the Green Mountain State, is situated in the New England region of the northeastern United States. Encompassing approximately nine thousand six hundred square miles as the sixth-smallest state by geographic area, Vermont is the only New England state without a coastline on the Atlantic Ocean. Its physical landscape is dominated by the north-south spine of the Green Mountains, a heavily forested ancient range of the Appalachian system, crowned by Mount Mansfield at four thousand three hundred and ninety-three feet. Along its western border lies the expansive Champlain Valley and the glacial waters of Lake Champlain, while the scenic Connecticut River forms the entirety of its eastern boundary with New Hampshire. Vermont features a heavily rural terrain characterized by narrow river valleys, rolling hardwood forests renowned for spectacular autumn foliage, and pristine upland pastures. Economically and culturally, Vermont pairs world-renowned specialty agriculture, leading the nation in pure maple syrup production alongside high-value artisanal dairy farming, with thriving four-season outdoor tourism, winter ski resorts, craft brewing, and extensive commercial quarrying of world-class marble and granite.
Kenya, officially the Republic of Kenya, occupies a strategic geographic crossroads in East Africa, bisected by the equator and bordered by the Indian Ocean to the southeast, Ethiopia and South Sudan to the north, Uganda to the west, Somalia to the northeast, and Tanzania to the south. Spanning approximately two hundred and twenty-four thousand square miles, Kenya features extraordinary topographical diversity anchored by the Great Rift Valley, a dramatic geological trench that cleaves the country from north to south, dotted by alkaline lakes and extinct volcanic calderas. To the east of the Rift Valley rise the fertile central highlands, culminating in the snow-dusted, glacier-carved crags of Mount Kenya, Africa's second-highest peak at seventeen thousand zero hundred and fifty-eight feet. Kenya slopes downward toward the tropical Indian Ocean coast, centered on the ancient maritime trade hub of Mombasa, while western Kenya embraces the fertile basin of Lake Victoria. Famed for its breathtaking wildlife biodiversity across the Maasai Mara savannah and vast national parks, Kenya combines a dominant agricultural economy in tea, coffee, and fresh horticulture with rapidly expanding geothermal energy generation and a vibrant technological and financial services hub centered in Nairobi.
